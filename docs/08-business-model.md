# 8. Offering User Testing Services as a Business

*Turning this capability into a service line a hub can sell.*

This section reflects the project team's observation that there is strong potential to replicate the approach as a service integrated into the portfolios of innovation hubs and business support intermediaries. It provides guidance on how these organizations can sustainably offer user testing as a commercial service beyond the lifetime of the project. The section is intended for hub leadership and programme managers who are considering whether, and how, to package and deliver user testing as a fee-based service.

## 8.1 Why This Is a Viable Service Line

The Project itself is the market signal. GIZ supported twelve Ghanaian organizations to build exactly this capability: structured user research, gender-sensitive facilitation, and evidence-based co-creation support, because Ghanaian solution providers do not have it in-house and cannot easily buy it elsewhere. That gap does not close when the project ends. If anything, the pipeline of early-stage digital solutions targeting informal-sector and women-led businesses in Ghana is growing, not shrinking, and most of those teams will face the same problem the solution providers faced: they are building for users they do not understand well enough, and they know it.

Two distinct buyer segments emerged from the project cohort, and a hub packaging this service should recognize both:

- **Under-resourced early-stage teams** (similar to several Phase 2 ideation teams) who need low-cost, fast testing to validate direction before they have built much: price-sensitive, but a natural funnel from a hub's own incubation or accelerator programme.
- **Better-resourced solution providers** (similar to several Phase 1 partners) who have a live product and need ongoing, rigorous testing and co-creation support to keep improving it: able to pay closer to market rate, but only if the hub can demonstrate it delivers findings a provider can act on, not just a report.

## 8.2 What a Hub Needs to Offer This Credibly

- **A trained facilitator, not just a UX-literate staff member.** [Section 4, Part A](04-design-practices.md#part-a-uiux-design-for-existing-solutions-phase-1) is effectively a training curriculum: user-centred design methods, task-based testing, neutral observation, gender-sensitive facilitation. A hub does not need a full-time UX team to start. It needs at least one person who has been through this discipline properly, plus a documented process others can follow.
- **A genuine community recruitment network.** This is the project's cohort's actual competitive advantage over a generic UX consultancy, and it is worth naming explicitly to clients: hubs with real relationships with market queens, savings groups, and trade associations can recruit representative, honest participants faster and more credibly than an outside firm can. This is the thing to sell, not just "we can run usability tests."
- **A reusable toolkit.** The screening template, observation sheet, and checklist in [Section 6](06-toolkit.md) are ready to use as-is. Standing up this service does not require building research instruments from scratch.
- **A track record.** Even one well-documented case study (ideally one the hub ran itself, not just this guide's project examples) does more to sell the service than any amount of description. Section 8.5 below explains how to build that case study.

## 8.3 Packaging the Service

There is no single right model. Four approaches showed up, implicitly or explicitly, across how the project itself was structured and how comparable hub-building efforts in Ghana (including incubation programmes built from scratch, such as the RUG Hub / RUG Incubation pattern) have introduced new service lines:

| Model | How it works | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed-scope sprint** | A defined engagement (e.g. one round of task-based testing with 8–10 participants, a findings report, and a prioritized recommendation list), priced and scoped upfront | First paying clients; building a portfolio quickly | Underscoping the recruitment effort, which is usually the most time-intensive part |
| **Retainer / ongoing co-creation partner** | The hub embeds with a solution provider across multiple test-adapt-retest cycles, mirroring Section 4's Step 1–3 loop | Better-resourced providers who need continuous improvement, not a one-off report | Requires the provider to actually have development capacity to act on findings (see Section 4, Part A, "When providers are reluctant, slow, or unable to change"); assess this before signing |
| **Bundled into an existing incubation or accelerator programme** | User testing is built into the cohort curriculum and fee structure rather than sold separately | Hubs that already run incubation programmes and want to raise the quality bar for graduating teams | Risk of treating it as a checkbox workshop rather than a real testing cycle with follow-through |
| **Grant- or donor-subsidized pilot cohort** | A funder (GIZ, another donor, or GHN itself) underwrites the first few engagements so the hub can build case studies without asking early clients to pay full price | Hubs with no track record yet | Needs a deliberate transition plan to paid engagements; subsidized pilots that never convert to revenue are not a service line, they are a grant |

Most hubs will want to start with a subsidized or discounted pilot, convert that into one or two fixed-scope sprints with documented outcomes, and only then consider a retainer model or bundling into a flagship programme.

## 8.4 Pricing Considerations in the Ghana Context

This guide does not set specific price points, since pricing structure will vary by hub capacity, region, and client type. Three considerations should shape whatever a hub decides:

- Most likely early clients, such as informal-sector-facing startups, NGO-backed pilots, other GHN-network solution providers, are cash-constrained in the same way the end users this guide describes are. Flat, transparent, pay-as-you-go pricing per engagement will land better than a subscription or retainer proposal to a team that has never bought this service before.
- **Segment pricing deliberately.** A venture-backed or foreign-funded client can reasonably be charged closer to market consulting rates; an informal-sector-facing early-stage team from the hub's own ecosystem may need a reduced or in-kind-for-equity/data-sharing arrangement, at least initially.
- **Use MoMo-based invoicing and payment where possible.** It is the payment method the hub's own future clients will find least friction in, consistent with [Section 6.2](06-toolkit.md#62-functional-design-patterns)'s evidence on payment infrastructure.
- **Expect to innovate on the payment model itself.** Solution providers rarely hold a budget line for user research, a point hubs across the GHN network have raised consistently. Models that spread or defer the cost, such as instalment payments by mobile money, milestone-based billing tied to co-creation cycles, or bundling research into a wider incubation package, widen the pool of clients who can realistically say yes.

## 8.5 Demonstrating Return on Investment

The strongest sales tool a hub has is a concrete before-and-after case study, quantified wherever possible. Two examples from this guide illustrate the format to reuse: Intermediary N1's Solution S1 password-simplification case ([Section 4, Part A, Step 3](04-design-practices.md#step-3-analysing-results-and-adapting-uiux)) shows a specific, low-cost fix moving login from a common failure point to near-universal success in one testing cycle. Intermediary A1's Solution S9 data ([Section 5.1](05-insights.md#51-digital-literacy-and-adoption-patterns)) shows the reverse: a concrete, previously-unknown failure rate (36.4% on a core task) that the provider could not have found without structured field testing.

A hub selling this service should be able to produce the same shape of evidence for its own first client within one engagement: a specific problem, a specific fix, a specific measured result. Generic claims about "improving usability" will not persuade a second client the way one real, numbered result will.

For early-stage clients there is a second return worth naming in the sales conversation: research participants are often a solution's first adopters. A well-recruited cohort that has shaped a product through several rounds of testing is also a ready-made group of early customers and references once the product launches.

!!! tip "Your first sales asset is your first client's own data, not a slide deck"
    A hub does not need a polished portfolio to start selling this service; it needs one completed engagement written up the way Section 4, Part A, Step 3 documents co-creation ("User feedback indicated [issue]. This led us to recommend [specific change]. Result: [measured outcome].") One real number beats ten generic claims.

## 8.6 Risks and What to Avoid

- **Do not sell testing without the follow-through capacity to support co-creation.** The project evidence is unambiguous (Section 4, Part A, "When providers are reluctant, slow, or unable to change"): testing that does not lead to acted-upon change produces no measurable benefit and damages the hub's credibility with that client. If a hub cannot also coach a client through prioritizing and implementing findings, it should say so upfront and scope the engagement accordingly.
- **Assess client readiness before selling**, the same way GFA had to assess solution-provider readiness before selection. A client with no development capacity to act on findings is not a good first paying client, however willing they are to pay for a report.
- **Do not oversell before running at least one real pilot.** Credibility in this market is built on demonstrated results, not marketing language (see Section 8.5).
- **Keep the evidence-based standard this guide holds itself to.** A hub's commercial pitch should be as honest about what testing can and cannot tell a client as this guide is about the project's own limitations ([Section 5.7](05-insights.md#57-limitations-and-ethical-considerations)).
