# 7. Recommendations

The following recommendations consolidate the most consistent findings from across the DTEG project's intermediary cohort. They synthesize the direct input of all participating organizations, organized by the audience most likely to act on each recommendation. Numbering within each list reflects the order intermediaries most commonly raised each point in their submissions, not a formal priority ranking — where this guide does intend a strict priority order (for example, which usability issues to fix first), it says so explicitly, as in Section 4, Part A, Step 3.

## 7.1 For Designers and Solution Providers

1. Test in the field, not the office. Lab or hub testing will not surface connectivity issues, environmental distractions, shared-device dynamics, or literacy barriers that appear in real market environments.
2. Prioritize language first. No other single improvement has as much impact on accessibility as making the core user journey available in the local language of the deployment region.
3. Offline functionality is not optional for peri-urban and rural deployment. Use local storage with background synchronization so that core functions work without an active internet connection.
4. Keep common actions fast. Two to three steps maximum for the most frequent tasks. Test this explicitly with real users under realistic conditions.
5. Invest in human support. An app users know they can call for help is used more than one that leaves them on their own.
6. Design for interruption. Real business environments are noisy and unpredictable. Tasks must be saveable and resumable without data loss.
7. Provide ready-made templates and pre-built structures for common business tasks.
8. Fix bugs before anything else. Unresolved bugs destroy trust among first-time users faster than any other issue.
9. Include women in ideation, not just testing. Participatory design workshops involving women micro-entrepreneurs produce fundamentally better products.
10. Consider differently-abled users explicitly. Gender-responsive design must go beyond the average user profile.
11. Avoid gender stereotypes in visual design. Choose colour, typography, and imagery based on context, readability, and trust, not gender assumptions.

## 7.2 For Intermediaries Running User Research

1. Invest in product knowledge before fieldwork. Intermediaries who understand the product deeply produce better findings.
2. Recruit through trusted community channels. Market queens, savings groups, and peer referral consistently outperform cold outreach.
3. Recruit more participants than the minimum. Attrition is predictable — build the buffer in from the start.
4. Recruit for diversity deliberately. Over-relying on literate participants hides significant usability gaps.
5. Design instruments for the participant, not the reporting template. Simple, conversational, visually supported tools produce better data.
6. Keep sessions short and focused. Fifteen to twenty minutes for questionnaires combined with task testing.
7. Maintain the same participant cohort through the full project lifecycle.
8. Link every recommendation to specific user evidence. Observation notes and direct quotes are what move solution providers to act.
9. Document what did not work. The record of failure is as valuable as the record of success.
10. Escalate early when solution providers are unresponsive.

## 7.3 For Project and Programme Managers

1. Assess solution provider readiness before selection. Evaluate development capacity, not just intent.
2. Build in enough time for co-creation to be substantive.
3. Make training attendance flexible.
4. Support intermediary organizations to continue this work beyond the project period — see Section 8 for how that can take the form of a paid service line.
5. Conduct post-project evaluation surveys that include solution providers.
6. Build solution-provider accountability into project design.
7. Monitor adoption after the project ends.
